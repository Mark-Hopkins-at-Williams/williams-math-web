#### Titles and abstracts

##### Session A – Morning – Wachenheim 113 (9:30am – 11:40am)

**Peter Fortin** (9:30am-10:10am)

*The Prime Correspondence Theorem for Localizations of Rings*

Abstract: There is a standard technique for constructing a field of fractions from a given integral domain. But for more general rings, the presence of zero divisors means that this technique fails to yield a well-defined multiplication operation. Localization is a powerful tool from commutative algebra which allows us to “cut out” the zeros of commutative rings with unity when forming their fractions. This method is foundational in algebraic geometry where the rings considered are polynomial rings over a field and their fractions are the corresponding rational functions. Localization allows us to focus in at a point where these rational functions are well defined, and we can then understand the global behavior of the ring by “gluing” together all such local data. The main result of this talk is the prime correspondence theorem: given a localization of a ring, there is an inclusion preserving bijection between the prime ideals of the ring which do not meet the set of denominators and the prime ideals of the localization itself.

**Niles Wahlin** (10:15am-10:55am)

*Reed–Solomon Error-Correction Codes: Why Scratchy CDs Still Play*

Abstract: Why does a scratched CD still play? The answer lies in error-correcting codes, which allow us to detect and fix errors in digital data. One of the most powerful and widely used is the Reed–Solomon code, which forms the backbone of technologies like CDs, QR codes, and space communications. We’ll begin with Irving S. Reed and Gustave Solomon’s 1960 foundational paper, *Polynomial Codes over Certain Finite Fields*, and build up key ideas behind the Reed–Solomon code from the bottom up.

**Aikedumanwen Izogie-Eghe** (11am-11:40am)

*The Rubik’s Cube: A Playground for Group Theory*

Abstract: The Rubik’s Cube is more than a puzzle. It’s a rich example of algebraic structure in action. In this talk, we’ll use the cube as a lens to explore fundamental concepts in group theory, beginning with definitions of groups, subgroups, and permutations. We’ll introduce the standard cube notation and then investigate how legal moves correspond to elements of a massive finite group. From there, we’ll calculate the order of the cube group and explain the constraints such as orientation and parity that limit its configurations. The core of the talk will be a group-theoretic description of the cube using a semidirect product. We’ll define semidirect products and explain why this decomposition captures the structure of corner and edge permutations and orientations. If time permits, we’ll discuss steps to solve the Rubik’s cube!

##### Session B – Morning – Wachenheim 114 (9:30am – 11:40am)

**Katie Scott** (9:30am-10:10am)

*The Birthday Problem and Beyond*

Abstract: The Classical Birthday Problem shows the surprising fact that in a room with 23 or more people, the probability of a shared birthday exceeds 50%. This counterintuitive result follows from the fact that collections of randomly chosen dates from a calendar have repetitions more often than we expect. We will present related results that shed light on the distribution of random independent events.

**Sammy Attada** (10:15am-10:55am)

*More Than Just a Game: The Mathematics of SET*

Abstract: The card game *SET* is governed by a simple visual rule, yet it hides a surprisingly rich mathematical structure. By translating cards into vectors over a finite field, we uncover connections between geometry, algebra, and probability. 

**Silas Fleissig** (11am-11:40am)

*Itô’s Lemma and Applications to Solving Stochastic Differential Equations*

Abstract: We present the foundations of stochastic calculus and derive Itô’s Lemma, which can be seen as an analog of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for stochastic processes. We use Itô’s Lemma to solve the stochastic differential equation satisfied by Geometric Brownian Motion and to link the expectation of a stochastic process to a partial differential equation, motivating the Feynman-Kac formula. These results have crucial applications in finance, physics, and biology.

##### Session A – Afternoon – Wachenheim 113 (1pm – 3:10pm)

**Roshni Pal** (1pm – 1:40pm)

*Why Savanna’s persist: A Mathematical Perspective on Fire and Vegetation Feedbacks*

Abstract: Savannas are ecosystems defined by the coexistence of grasses and a discontinuous tree canopy, yet classical ecological models often treat them as transient states maintained by externally imposed disturbances such as fire. This talk presents a disturbance-mediated mathematical model demonstrating how savannas can emerge as stable ecological states through feedbacks between vegetation and fire. The model consists of a system of coupled ordinary differential equations describing grasses, fire-tolerant savanna trees, and fire-intolerant forest trees. After nondimensionalization, equilibrium solutions and their local stability are analyzed using Jacobian methods and supported by numerical simulations. The results show that grass-driven fire feedbacks destabilize savannas, while fire facilitation by savanna trees generates self-limiting dynamics that stabilize coexistence.

**Ryan Guo** (1:45pm – 2:25pm)

*When do Irrational Numbers Repeat? An Introduction to Continued Fractions*

Abstract: If we write the square root of 2 as a decimal, we get a seemingly random, non-repeating stream of digits: 1.41421356… However, if we view this number through the lens of continued fractions, a stunningly simple pattern emerges: sqrt[2] = 1+1/(2+1/(2+ 1/(2+ 1/(…)))) Why does this hidden structure exist for some numbers but not others? This talk will introduce the theory of continued fractions, a tool that offers a far more revealing structure for real numbers than standard decimal notation. We will begin with a visual, geometric interpretation of the Euclidean algorithm to build an intuition for how these fractions are constructed. From there, we will derive the fundamental recurrence relations for convergents – the sequence of rational numbers that provide the “best possible” approximations of an irrational number. Finally, we will prove a beautiful classification theorem (Lagrange’s theorem on the periodicity of continued fractions) that answers our opening question: a continued fraction has a periodic expansion if and only if the number is a quadratic irrational (a solution to a quadratic equation with integer coefficients). Whether you are interested in number theory, approximation algorithms, or simply finding order in mathematical chaos, this talk will change the way you visualize the real number line. 

**Forrest Hu** (2:30pm – 3:10pm)

*Representations, Gauge Theory, and the Standard Model of Particle Physics*

Abstract: This colloquium addresses the question: how do we use mathematical objects to describe the elementary particles and forces that constitute the natural world? The correct approach is to classify particles according to how they transform under the symmetry operations (rotations, translations, or acceleration) of the Poincaré group. We study the irreducible representations of the Poincaré group through the method of Lie algebras and outline some of the consequences of the theory, possibly including the Dirac theory of the electron, the spin statistics theorem, and the need for quantum fields. If time permits, we will also talk about gauge theory and the interpretation of fundamental forces as curvature in an abstract geometric space.

##### Session B – Afternoon – Wachenheim 114 (1pm – 3:10pm)

**Jamie Tang** (1pm – 1:40pm)

*Gauss’s Theorema Egregium and Its Applications *

Abstract: We define the first and second fundamental forms associated with a parametrization of a regular surface. This gives us the necessary machinery to define the Gaussian curvature and prove Gauss’s celebrated Theorema Egregium, which establishes that the Gaussian curvature is invariant under local isometries; hence, it’s an intrinsic property of a surface independent of its embedding. Finally, we explore the applications of this result to various geometries. 

**Yasemin Sukal** (1:45pm – 2:25pm)

*Cut, Rearrange, Repeat: When Does Shape Equal Shape?*

Abstract: What does it mean for two shapes to be “the same” if we are allowed to cut them apart and rearrange the pieces? This question leads us to scissors congruence, an equivalence relation on polygons generated by dissecting them into finitely many pieces and reassembling those pieces. In this talk, we look at the Wallace-Bolyai-Gerwien theorem, which gives an elegant answer to our question. The proof is entirely geometric, built from simple shapes hiding inside complicated ones.

**Janne Koch** (2:30pm – 3:10pm)

*Momma Worm Goes Blanket Shopping: The Search for the Smallest Convex Cover*

Abstract: The Baby Worm can curl up in any position, straight, wavy, or spiraled, and Momma Worm needs a blanket that covers them all. We search for the smallest convex region that guarantees Baby Worm/ unit length curve is covered.
