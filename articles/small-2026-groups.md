#### Tiling Theory

**Advisor:** Colin Adams

**Project Description:**

The theory of tilings dates back to when people first began to decorate their buildings with tiles. This summer, we will be researching tilings of the sphere and other related spaces.

The classification of all of the possible tilings of the sphere by regular spherical polygons glued edge-to-edge was completed by work of Johnson and Zalgaller in 1967. The tilings include the Platonic tilings, the Archimedean tilings, the prism and antiprism tilings and the 25 Johnson tilings. The non-edge-to-edge tilings were classified by myself and students in 2021 <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.10743>, thereby completing the task of identifying all tilings of the sphere by regular spherical polygons. In the process, we found some interesting new tilings, including the kaleidoscope tilings, the lunar tilings, the composed tilings, and the sporadic tilings.

This summer, we will look at tilings of the sphere and related spaces, and consider variations on regular spherical polygonal tilings.

#### Modeling the Role of Inhibitory Interneurons in Cortical Network Dynamics

**Advisors:** Christina Athanasouli and Madeline Parker

**Project Description:**

This project will explore how different types of inhibitory interneurons – parvalbumin (PV), somatostatin (SOM), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) cells – shape the activity of cortical networks. These interneurons are known to regulate synchrony and firing rates in neural populations, but many questions remain about their individual and collective contributions to network function, especially during shifts in brain state such as arousal or locomotion.

The project builds on prior computational modeling work that extended exponential integrate-and-fire neuron frameworks to include multiple interneuron subtypes in spatially structured networks. Previous studies and modeling suggest, for example, that stimulating PV neurons can enhance gamma oscillations, while SOM neurons play a key role in regulating transitions between synchronous and asynchronous states. By developing and analyzing computational models, this project will allow undergraduates to study how these cell types influence network stability, synchrony, and response to external inputs.

Potential questions to investigate involve topics such as:

1. *Incorporating realistic biological features*: How do factors such as within-population heterogeneity or synaptic plasticity alter the dynamics observed?
2. *Underlying neural model*: What happens if the base model is changed to a different neuron type? How does this affect the dynamics originally captured in the exponential integrate-and-fire? Do specific models exhibit more realistic features?
3. *Spike train replication*: What information is encoded in a spike train? Can network connections be artificially reproduced using a statistically analogous, synthetic spike train imposed on the network and replicate full network connectivity?
4. *Input types to match real-world stimulus*: How do different kinds of external input drive network dynamics? What are the most realistic stimuli to match experimentally observed findings?

These explorations will provide insight into fundamental principles of brain function while introducing undergraduates to advanced methods in computational neuroscience, including dynamical systems analysis, simulation, and data-driven modeling.

#### Number Theory and Probability

**Advisor:** Steven J. Miller (and possibly [Eyvi Palsson](https://math.vt.edu/people/faculty/palsson-eyvindur.html) and others)

**Project Description:**

We’ll explore many of the interplays between number theory and probability / harmonic analysis, with projects drawn from L-functions, Random Matrix Theory, Additive Number Theory (such as the 3x+1 Problem and Zeckendorf decompositions), and Benford’s law. A common theme in many of these systems is either a probabilistic model or heuristic. For example, Random Matrix Theory was developed to study the energy levels of heavy nuclei. While it can be hard to analyze the behavior of a specific configuration it is often possible to say something about the configurations in aggregate. For instance, it is often easy to calculate an average over all configurations, and then appeal to a Central Limit Theorem type result to say that a generic systems behavior is close to this average. These techniques have been applied to many problems, ranging from the behavior of L-functions to the structure of networks to city transportation.

The choice of problems will be chosen by student interest from these and other related topics. For references for each set of problems and additional details, please go to <http://www.williams.edu/Mathematics/sjmiller/public_html/ntprob19> , and you can access my papers at my homepage <https://web.williams.edu/Mathematics/sjmiller/public_html/math/papers/papers.html> as well as videos and slides for talks at [https://web.williams.edu/Mathematics/sjmiller/public\_html/math/talks/talks.hml](https://web.williams.edu/Mathematics/sjmiller/public_html/math/talks/talks.html)

#### Ergodic Theory

**Advisor:** Cesar E. Silva

**Project Description:**

Ergodic theory studies the probabilistic or measurable behavior of abstract dynamical systems. Dynamical systems are systems that change with time, such as the motion of the planets or of a pendulum. Abstract dynamical systems represent the state of a dynamical system by a point in a mathematical space (phase space). One typically assumes that time is measured at discrete intervals and so the law of motion of the system is represented by a single self-map of the phase space. One of the main areas in ergodic theory is the study (from the probabilistic or measurable viewpoint) of maps of the unit interval to itself. Such maps can be seen as actions of the group of integers on the interval; one may also be interested in actions of other groups such as the  2-dimensional integer lattice group.

One area of ergodic theory is the study of various dynamical behaviors such as ergodicity, weak mixing and mixing.  We will be interested in constructing examples and counterexamples of abstract dynamical systems satisfying these various properties.
